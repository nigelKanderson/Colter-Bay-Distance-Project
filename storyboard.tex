\section{Overview}
% ============================================================

Study question: does light colour (red vs. white) affect bat acoustic
activity, and does the answer depend on intensity or distance from the
source?

Design: 14 acoustic monitoring stations along a distance gradient
($<$0.52 km to $>$2.1 km from source), red vs. white light crossed
at five intensities (10, 30, 50, 70, 100\%). Survey season: 2022.
Eight focal species: Epfu, Laci, Lano, Myev, Mylu, Myvo, Myyu, Myci.

This report extends the design in two directions. First, the six
species with sufficient data are further characterized by four
continuous wing-morphology traits (ear height, forearm length, wing
loading, aspect ratio) in place of a discrete foraging guild (Section
\ref{sec:traits}). Second, the same light treatments were run past
moth (Lepidoptera) camera traps at all 14 stations over the same
season, identified to family/subfamily, allowing a direct bat-vs-moth
comparison of the light color, intensity, and distance effects
(Sections \ref{sec:moths}--\ref{sec:comparison}).

Figures are limited to the main results in the body of this report;
additional supporting figures are collected in Supplemental Materials
at the end.

\section{Bat Morphology Traits}
\label{sec:traits}
% ============================================================

Foraging guild (Open-air / Edge / Clutter) was replaced with four
continuous wing-morphology measurements per species: ear height,
forearm length, wing loading, and aspect ratio. Each trait was modeled
separately (z-scored, interacted with intensity and distance) and
compared at representative low / mean / high ($\pm$1 SD) values, in
place of the three discrete guild levels.

All four traits point the same direction. Species with smaller ears,
shorter forearms, lower wing loading, and lower aspect ratio ---
slower, more maneuverable fliers such as \textit{Myotis} spp. --- show
a steeper increase in activity with distance from the light source,
while species at the high end of each trait (larger, faster,
open-air-adapted fliers such as Laci) are comparatively flat across the
same gradient. The same ordering holds for intensity: low-trait-value
species have consistently higher predicted detections at every
intensity level, with the gap narrowing at high intensity. Because
forearm length, wing loading, ear height, and aspect ratio are all
measured on the same six species, the four traits are highly
correlated with one another (and with species identity itself, as
guild was) --- the four panels are best read as one wing-morphology
gradient viewed four ways, not four independent effects
(intensity view in Supplemental Fig.\ S11).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_trait_dist.png}
  \caption{Predicted bat detections across the distance gradient,
  evaluated at low/mean/high ($\pm$1 SD) values of each morphology
  trait. Low-trait-value species (smaller, slower, more maneuverable)

\subsection{Data and General Activity Model}

Moth specimens were identified from nightly camera-trap photographs at
the same 14 monitoring stations, over the same 2022 season (21 June --
16 August). Each photo's filename encoded site, date, and light
treatment (e.g.\ \texttt{GRTE01\_2aug22\_w100D}); intensity readings
that deviated from the bat project's 10/30/50/70/100\% schedule were
rounded to the nearest level, and repeat photos of the same
site/date/treatment were merged. 9{,}864 identified specimens across
241 photographs (225 site-nights) were retained, spanning 11 families
and 31 subfamilies, dominated numerically by Noctuidae, Lasiocampidae,
and Erebidae.

A general activity model --- mirroring the bat Primary Model exactly
(color $\times$ intensity + intensity $\times$ dist\_km + mean\_phase +
pct\_nonforest + jd\_c + jd\_c$^2$ + (1$\vert$site), negative binomial,
zero-inflated) --- was fit to total nightly moth counts.

\subsection{Family-Level Models}

Six of nine families cleared the same 30-detection / 20-row threshold
used for the bat species models (Noctuidae, Lasiocampidae, Erebidae,
Geometridae, Sphingidae, Notodontidae); Crambidae, Drepanidae, and
Euteliidae had too few identified specimens to model individually.
Noctuidae is numerically dominant (predicted 40--50 detections/night
vs. $<$15 for every other family) and drives most of the aggregate
pattern above. At 100\% intensity, Erebidae, Noctuidae, and Geometridae
each show a 2--3$\times$ white-over-red ratio; at lower intensities the
families track each other closely with wide, overlapping confidence
intervals (Supplemental Figs.\ S15--S16).

distance signal primarily to Lasiocampidae (p $<$ 2e-16) and Noctuidae
(p = 0.005) (Supplemental Figs.\ S17--S18).

The two taxa diverge most clearly on light color: averaged across
intensity, bats show 22\% fewer detections under white than red (CI
excludes 1), while moths show 24\% more (CI excludes 1) --- opposite
directions. At 100\% intensity specifically, bat activity is
indistinguishable from the 10\% baseline (ratio 1.03, ns) while moth
activity drops significantly (ratio 0.68, CI excludes 1): bats hold
steady at maximum brightness where moths pull back. Both taxa increase
with distance from the light source over the shared 0--7.4 km range,
and the relative increase is if anything larger for moths (+125\% at
the far end vs.\ +80\% for bats); only the moth slope is significant on
its own (CI excludes 1), because the bat's marginal, intensity-averaged
slope is diluted by the reversal at 70--100\% intensity documented in
the Primary Model (Section 2) --- a reversal not present in the moth
data.

% ============================================================
\section{Summary}
% ============================================================

1. Colour $\times$ intensity interaction: red light at 30--50\% produced
   1.6--2.1$\times$ more detections than white. At 70\% and 100\%,
   colours converge. White light is flat across all intensities.

2. Intensity $\times$ distance interaction: 70\% and 100\% intensities
   show significant negative distance slopes (intensity70:dist\_km
   $\beta$ = $-$0.128, p $<$ 0.001; intensity100:dist\_km $\beta$ =
   $-$0.090, p = 0.001), meaning detections under high intensity
   increase with distance from source. 30\% and 50\% slopes are
   non-significant --- low intensities are distance-invariant.

3. Community composition is driven by distance, habitat (pct\_nonforest),
   and sky darkness --- not by light colour.

4. Wing morphology (ear height, forearm length, wing loading, aspect
   ratio) replaces guild as the trait-level lens: smaller, slower,
   more maneuverable species consistently show higher and more
   distance-sensitive activity than larger, faster, open-air fliers,
   across every intensity level.

5. Moths mirror bats on intensity and distance (both show a positive
   distance effect that weakens at 100\% intensity, and light color
   has no effect on community composition in either taxon) but invert
   the color effect: bats favor red overall while moths favor white,
   most sharply at 100\% intensity (bat ratio 1.03 ns vs.\ moth ratio
   0.68, CI excludes 1).

6. Management implication: red light at 30--50\% intensity minimises
   suppression of bat activity while also avoiding the white-light
   attraction effect seen in moths at high intensity; siting lights
   $>$0.5--1.0 km from sensitive habitat remains important for both
   taxa at high intensity.
